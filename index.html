Lights Out: 31 Seasons of F1, Lap by Lap

By Emily Schallenberg · Financial Data Analytics · Data Website Project

This report studies every timed lap of every Formula 1 World Championship race from 1996 to the Azerbaijan Grand Prix on 26 September 2026: 632,937 laps across 583 Grands Prix, run by 147 drivers for 45 constructors. Each lap records who drove it, for which team, their running position at the end of the lap and the lap time. The data shows that F1 is ruled by eras. One team led more than 80% of all laps in five different seasons, and Lewis Hamilton alone led more than one lap in every seven. Whoever leads after lap 1 wins more than half the time. Along the way, the sport changed shape: starters went from finishing 57% of the time to 93%, the calendar grew from 16 races to 24, and rule changes such as DRS moved the number of on-track place changes far more than anything the drivers did. The 2026 season, the first under new engine and chassis rules, is still under way; it is included through its 15th race and marked as partial wherever it appears.

Lap 01 · Laps led

Lewis Hamilton led 5,531 laps, more than one in every seven raced since 1996

Every race lap has exactly one leader, so the 583 races in the data contain 35,546 laps led. Lewis Hamilton led 5,531 of them (15.6%), 1,594 more than Michael Schumacher (3,937), even though Schumacher's first five championship seasons fall before this data begins.

Leading is concentrated at the very top. Hamilton, Schumacher, Max Verstappen (3,883) and Sebastian Vettel (3,501) together led 16,852 laps, or 47.4% of all laps raced since 1996. Verstappen passed Vettel for third in 2025 and is now just 54 laps behind Schumacher. Fernando Alonso (1,773) and Charles Leclerc (1,000) are the other drivers in the top 10 still racing in 2026.

Lap 02 · Dominance

Red Bull's 2023 season was the most one-sided since 1996: 86.7% of laps led

For each season, the chart shows the constructor that led the most laps and its share of that season's laps. The seasons run in clear eras: Williams (1996–97), McLaren (1998–99), Ferrari (2000–2004), Red Bull (2010–2013), Mercedes (2014–2020) and Red Bull again (2021–2024). McLaren topped 2025 (57.1%), and Mercedes leads 2026 so far with 65.2% of the laps run.

Red Bull led 1,149 of 1,325 laps in 2023 (86.7%), just ahead of Mercedes in 2014 (980 laps, 86.3%) and 2016 (83.2%). The closest seasons were 2012, when Red Bull led only 36.4%, and 2003, when Ferrari led 37.6%. Mercedes topped the table for seven straight years, the longest run in the data.

Lap 03 · The start

The leader after lap 1 goes on to win 56% of races

The driver in first place at the end of lap 1 won 325 of 583 races (55.7%). Second place after lap 1 won 23.0% of the time, and third place 9.8%. From fourth place on, the odds collapse: only 67 races (11.5%) were won by a driver running fourth or lower after the first lap, and just 9 by a driver outside the top 10.

Lap 1 position is used instead of grid position because the data records positions at the end of each lap. It already includes the start and the first corners, where many races are decided.

Lap 04 · Reliability

Finishing became the norm: 57% of starters were classified in 1996, 93% in 2024

A driver is classified when they complete at least 90% of the winner's laps (the FIA rule). In 1996 only 176 of 310 starts (56.8%) were classified. By 2024 that rose to 432 of 465 (92.9%). The rate climbed steadily through the early 2000s and passed 90% for the first time in 2021 (91.6%), and 2025 was almost as high (92.5%). The first 15 races under the new 2026 rules have dipped to 82.5% (264 of 320 starts), the lowest since 2010 (79.7%).

The biggest single jump came in 2004–2005 (from 69.7% in 2003 to 76.4% and then 80.0%), when rules started forcing engines to last a whole race weekend and then two. Over all 31 seasons, 80.0% of starts ended classified.

Lap 05 · Racing

DRS and fast-wearing tyres made 2011–2013 the busiest era for changes of position

Counting every place a driver gains from one lap to the next (the start is excluded) gives a measure of how much the order changes during a race. From 1996 to 2010 a race averaged 149 places gained. In 2011 the sport added DRS (a rear wing that opens on straights) and Pirelli's deliberately fast-wearing tyres. The average jumped to 248 per race in 2011–2013, peaking at 253.8 in 2012.

The count dropped to its lowest point, 108.4 in 2017, the first year of wider, high-downforce cars that struggle to follow each other. It recovered to 186.4 in 2023 under the 2022 ground-effect rules, was 161.8 in 2025, and stands at 137.2 for the new 2026 cars so far. This count includes places swapped during pit stops, so it measures how much the order changes rather than strictly overtakes.

Lap 06 · Speed

At Monaco, 2014's hybrid cars were 4 seconds a lap slower than 2004's V10s

Monaco's street circuit has barely changed since 1996, which makes it a good stopwatch for the cars themselves. The fastest race lap fell from 1:25.205 in 1996 to 1:14.439 in 2004, the peak of the V10 era. The 2014 switch to 1.6-litre hybrid engines and narrower cars pushed it back up to 1:18.479, 4.04 seconds slower.

The wider 2017 cars won the time back straight away (1:14.820), and the fastest Monaco race lap in the data is 1:12.909, set in 2021; 2025 (1:13.221) and 2026 (1:13.481) are the next fastest. Race laps depend on fuel load, tyres and weather. The 1997 race was run in heavy rain and was stopped at the two-hour time limit after 62 of its 78 scheduled laps, so its fastest lap was 1:53.315, more than 30 seconds slower than the dry 1998 race (1:22.948). That point is marked at the top edge of the chart instead of being drawn to scale. Monaco was not held in 2020 because of the pandemic.

Lap 07 · The calendar

The season grew from 16 races to 24, and the field ran 85% more laps

There were 16 Grands Prix in 1996 and 24 in both 2024 and 2025, the record. Most of the growth came after 2015 (19 races), with new rounds in Azerbaijan, Saudi Arabia, Qatar, Miami and Las Vegas. The 2020 season was cut to 17 races by the pandemic.

More races and fuller finishing fields add up: the drivers together completed 14,431 laps in 1996 and 26,657 in 2025, 84.7% more. The 2026 season has had 15 races so far.

Lap 08 · Nationality

British and German drivers won 56% of all races

By the nationality of the winning driver, Britain now leads with 167 wins (19 drivers, led by Hamilton, with Lando Norris and George Russell adding 14 in 2025–2026) and Germany follows with 158 (13 drivers, led by Schumacher and Vettel). Together they won 325 of the 583 races (55.7%).

The Netherlands is third with 71 wins, all by one driver: Max Verstappen. Five other Dutch drivers raced in this period without winning. Finland's 52 wins came from four drivers: Räikkönen (21), Häkkinen (20), Bottas (10) and Kovalainen (1).

Lap 09 · Team lineage

Four team lineages won 89% of races; Haas has yet to win one

F1 teams change names when they change owners, so the report groups each constructor under the team it became on the 2025 grid. For example, Tyrrell → BAR → Honda → Brawn → Mercedes, and Stewart → Jaguar → Red Bull. On that basis Ferrari leads with 146 wins, followed by the Mercedes lineage (141) and the Red Bull lineage (131).

Add McLaren's 101 and those four lineages account for 519 of 583 wins (89.0%). The Enstone team (Benetton → Renault → Lotus → Alpine) won 24 and Williams 31, most of them in the 1990s. Haas has 443 race starts since 2016 and has never led a lap. Cadillac joined in 2026 as the first new team since Haas.

Pit wall · About the data

Where the numbers come from

Source
1996–2024: data/formula1file.xlsx, sheet f1_lap_times_panel, compiled from the Ergast Motor Racing database (lap times, races, drivers and constructors tables; the IDs follow Ergast's numbering), which has timed laps for every championship race from 1996 on. 2025–2026: data/laps_2025_2026.csv, the same columns fetched from the Jolpica-F1 API, which continues the Ergast database (data licensed CC BY-NC-SA 4.0), by scripts/fetch_new_seasons.py.
One row
One driver on one lap of one race, with 14 columns (season, round, race, date, driver, code, nationality, constructor, lap number, position at the end of the lap and lap time): 589,081 rows from the spreadsheet plus 43,856 for 2025–2026, 632,937 in all. There are 583 races and 147 drivers over 31 seasons.
Rows dropped
None. Neither file has missing values or duplicate driver-laps. It only holds laps that were completed, so a driver who retired before finishing lap 1 does not appear. Very long laps (red flags, repairs, pit stops) are kept. They affect only lap-time measures, which use the fastest or median lap.
Driver-race table
scripts/build_data.py combines the two files, matching drivers by name (the two sources number them differently), and rolls the laps up to one row per driver per race (11,826 rows, data/driver_races.csv). The dashboard loads this table, and every report figure is computed from it (data/report_data.json).
Laps led
Laps completed in position 1. Each race lap has one leader, so all laps led add up to 35,546 race laps.
Partial season
2026 is included through round 15, the Azerbaijan Grand Prix on 26 September 2026, and is marked with an asterisk in per-season charts. Its totals (races, laps) are not comparable with complete seasons; its rates and averages are.
Share of laps led
Laps led ÷ all race laps in the selected seasons and Grands Prix, whether or not the driver or team completed a lap in every race. For one season, this is laps led ÷ all laps of that season.
Win
In position 1 at the end of the race's final lap: first across the line. Post-race penalties are not in the data, so two official results differ: Vettel is counted for Canada 2019 (Hamilton was awarded it) and Russell for Belgium 2024 (Hamilton was awarded it after Russell's disqualification). Every 2025 winner in the data matches the official result; 2026 results are as published by Jolpica-F1.
Classified / finish rate
Completed at least 90% of the race's laps, rounded down. Finish rate = classified ÷ driver-race entries.
Podium
Classified and in the top 3 on the last lap completed.
Win rate
Wins ÷ driver-race entries.
Lap 1 position
Position at the end of lap 1 (the data has no starting grid).
Places gained on track
For each lap from lap 2 on, the positions a driver moved up since the previous lap (drops count as zero), summed. Per race = the total ÷ the number of races. Includes places changed during pit stops.
Fastest / median lap
The minimum of all a driver's lap times, and the median lap of each driver-race.
Team lineage
Named as on the 2025 grid: Ferrari; McLaren; Williams; Red Bull (Stewart, Jaguar); Mercedes (Tyrrell, BAR, Honda, Brawn); Aston Martin (Jordan, MF1, Spyker, Force India, Racing Point); Alpine (Benetton, Renault, Lotus F1); Haas; Sauber (BMW Sauber, Alfa Romeo, and Audi from 2026); VCARB (Minardi, Toro Rosso, AlphaTauri, RB); and Cadillac, new in 2026 with no predecessor. Teams with no successor on the grid (Ligier, Prost, Footwork, Arrows, Forti, Toyota, Super Aguri, Lotus 2010–11, Caterham, Virgin, Marussia, Manor, HRT) are grouped as Defunct.
